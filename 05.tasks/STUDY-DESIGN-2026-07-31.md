# Redrafted study design, 2026-07-31

Supersedes the soil-carbon-only framing. Written after reading the seven papers deposited in
`04.references/literature/`. Target venue remains **Global Change Biology**.

## The question

Does invasion increase forest carbon sequestration?

## The answer the literature already contains, and nobody has stated

**Invasion consistently increases the rate of carbon accumulation. It does not reliably increase
the carbon stock.** The field disagrees about invasion and carbon because it has been measuring
two different quantities and calling both "sequestration".

This is not a semantic point. It is the distinction between the two methods the IPCC defines as
"equally valid" for estimating land carbon change: the **gain-loss method**, which sums annual
increment against annual losses (2006 Guidelines Vol. 4 Eq. 2.4), and the **stock-difference
method**, which differences measured stocks at two times (Eq. 2.5). Applied to invaded land the
two methods disagree in sign, and carbon crediting pays for the one that shows no effect.

### Evidence that the rate rises

| Source | System | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Pati et al. 2025 | *Gliricidia sepium*, dry deciduous secondary forest, Central India | Sequestration rate **8.01 +/- 1.50 against 0.71 +/- 2.39 Mg C/ha/yr**, an 11.3-fold difference, while the biomass stock differs by only 1.3-fold (94.45 +/- 10.27 against 72.85 +/- 9.50 Mg/ha). The divergence appears within one study |
| Liao et al. 2008 | Global, 94 studies | Fluxes, including ANPP and litter decomposition, **up 50 to 120%** |
| Wardle et al. 2007 | 18 New Zealand islands | Invasive rats, via seabird predation, raise live plant biomass C **+104%** |
| Meitha et al. 2024 | *Leucaena*, Taiwanese badlands | Root-derived carbon argued to form mineral-associated soil carbon more efficiently than litter |

### Evidence that the stock does not

| Source | Result |
|---|---|
| This project's meta-analysis | Soil organic carbon on an areal basis **−0.8%** (95% CI −15.7 to +16.7) |
| Lázaro-Lobo et al. 2025 | Soil organic carbon **greater under native** forests, 1,678 case studies |
| Xu et al. 2022 | **No significant effect** on soil organic carbon in vegetated native wetlands |
| Nagy et al. 2021 | Total soil carbon **unchanged**; biomass and litter down 76 g C/m² |
| Wardle et al. 2007 | Non-living pools **−26%**, partly offsetting the +104% in live biomass |

### The mechanism is already published

Peltzer et al. 2010, the canonical review and the paper this design is built around, states the
principle:

> the direct effects of invaders on forest C are often smaller and shorter-term than their
> indirect effects caused by altered nutrient availability, primary productivity or species
> composition, all of which regulate long-term C pools and fluxes

and concludes:

> Without knowing the long-term C trajectories of an invaded system, it will be difficult to
> demonstrate gains in C as a result of management.

A fast-growing invader raises the rate immediately and visibly. Whether that translates into a
durable stock depends on residence time, which is governed by indirect effects operating over
decades. Rate is measurable in a season; stock is not.

## Study design

**Part 1, the rate-stock divergence.** Compile paired invaded and uninvaded observations that
report a **rate** (net primary production, increment, sequestration rate) and, separately, those
reporting a **stock** (biomass carbon, soil carbon). Estimate pooled log response ratios for each
class and test whether the difference between them is significant. Prediction: rate effects are
large and positive, stock effects are near zero, and the contrast is significant.

**Part 2, trophic generality.** Peltzer et al. organise invader effects by trophic level:
primary producers, decomposers, herbivores, pathogens, mutualists and predators. Wardle et al.
demonstrate that an invasive **predator** alters ecosystem carbon by +37% through a trophic
cascade with no plant invasion at all. Partition the compiled effects by invader trophic level.
Prediction: the rate-stock divergence is not confined to plant invaders.

**Part 3, the accounting consequence.** Apply the two IPCC methods to the same compiled evidence
and show where they disagree. The IPCC's own guidance supplies the condition: stock-difference is
favoured for large changes and fails "where biomass change is very small compared to the total
amount of biomass" (Vol. 4 Ch. 4, p. 4.11). Invasion is exactly that case, a high rate against a
large standing stock.

**Part 4, the policy risk.** Lindenmayer et al. 2012 name three bio-perversities from carbon
programmes, of which the second is planting trees that become invasive. Pati et al. conclude that
planting invasive species "might create obstruction to achieve the goals of REDD+ and UN
resolutions on ecosystem restoration". Nunes et al. and Meitha et al. argue the opposite case,
that invader biomass should be valorised or retained. Set the rate-stock result against this
dispute: crediting rewards rate-like evidence, and the durable stock may not follow.

## Why this is publishable

It resolves a live disagreement rather than adding to it, using a distinction that is already
formalised in international accounting guidance but has never been applied to invasion. It
explains why two literatures that both look careful reach opposite conclusions. It generalises
beyond plants. And it identifies a specific, consequential error: sequestration rate is being
used as evidence for stock gain in a policy setting that pays for stock.

## Corrections to the deposited literature

1. **"Kumar et al 2025" is Pati et al. 2025.** First author Pranab Kumar Pati; "Kumar" is a
   middle name. *Environmental Challenges* 20:101186, doi:10.1016/j.envc.2025.101186. Filename

2. **"Lindenmayer et al 2023" is Lindenmayer et al. 2012.** *Conservation Letters* 5(1):28-36,
   doi:10.1111/j.1755-263X.2011.00213.x. Received 2011, accepted November 2011.
3. **Wang 2023 is published in the National High School Journal of Science and must not be
   cited.** It is a secondary-school student publication, carries no DOI, and is not peer

   show 3.1 times higher net primary production, is attributed to its own reference 47 and
   should be traced to that primary source instead. Citing it in *Global Change Biology* would
   be damaging.

## What carries over

The completed soil carbon meta-analysis becomes Part 1's stock limb rather than the whole paper.
The measurement-basis finding, that the apparent soil carbon gain exists only in concentration
data and vanishes in areal stocks, survives intact and strengthens the argument: it is a second
instance of the same error, measuring one quantity and reporting another. The standards audit
and the IPCC accounting evidence become Part 3.


## Correction recorded 2026-08-01

An earlier draft of this document misread Pati et al.'s Table 1. The PDF text layer renders the
plus-or-minus sign as an arrow, and the paired values were taken for invaded-against-uninvaded
comparisons when they are in fact mean and standard error for a single community. The correct
readings are: total biomass 94.45 +/- 10.27 (invaded) against 72.85 +/- 9.50 (uninvaded) Mg/ha,
and carbon sequestration rate 8.01 +/- 1.50 against 0.71 +/- 2.39 Mg C/ha/yr. Stem density
1059.2 +/- 37.94 is the invaded community alone.

The correction strengthens the design rather than weakening it. Within this single study the
biomass stock differs by 30 per cent while the sequestration rate differs by more than a
thousand per cent, which is the rate-stock divergence demonstrated on one set of plots. Note
also that the uninvaded rate has a standard error larger than its mean and is therefore not
distinguishable from zero.

**Lesson for the pipeline: never read a paired value from a PDF text layer without confirming
whether the separator is a comparison or a dispersion term.**
